The brief, restated
A box-office counter needs to price a booking correctly, every time, and show its work — where "correctly" means the messy parts (sold-out tiers, stacked offers, fees, slab-based tax, a dirty upstream price feed) all resolve to a bill that adds up to the exact paisa. I built this in the order the problem implied: get a plain booking total right first, then layer complexity on top of something that already works, rather than trying to get the whole thing right in one pass.

Why Decimal, and why paisa-exactness is the hard part
Money bugs are almost never in the arithmetic — they're in rounding happening at the wrong point, or a discount being computed once but displayed as if it applied evenly to two different line items. Two decisions eliminate that class of bug entirely:

Everything is Decimal, quantized to 2 places (the paisa), immediately after every arithmetic operation. No floats anywhere. This alone prevents the classic 0.1 + 0.2 class of error, but it doesn't solve the harder problem below.
Discount allocation uses a largest-remainder split in integer paise, not proportional division. When a combined ₹150 discount has to be split across a Silver line and a Gold line by their share of the gross, naive proportional division (total * weight / sum) produces fractional paise that don't sum back to exactly ₹150 once each line is independently rounded. The fix: compute each line's exact integer-paisa share and remainder, hand out the leftover paise one at a time to the lines with the largest remainders, and only then convert back to Decimal. This guarantees sum(line_discounts) == total_discount on every single booking, not just the ones where the math happens to round cleanly. The same technique is the right tool anywhere a total has to be split into parts that must re-sum exactly (it's the same method used for apportioning seats in parliamentary systems, for exactly this reason).
Line-item GST is computed per tier, after discount allocation, and then summed — never computed once on the grand total and then divided back out. That ordering is what makes the itemized bill trustworthy: every number on the receipt is a real, independently-computed quantity, not a display-only estimate that happens to be consistent with the total.

Why offers stack in a specific order
The brief describes two offer types: a flat festival discount and a capped percentage member discount. I chose to apply the flat discount first, then the percentage discount on what's left, because that's how real box offices generally frame "extra X% off after your festival savings" promotions — the percentage is meant to bite on the already-discounted amount, not the original gross. This is a genuine policy decision, not a computational necessity (applying them in the other order changes the final total when the percentage discount would otherwise get capped), so it's implemented as one clear, commented step rather than buried in a formula, and would be a one-line change to flip if a given cinema's policy differs.

Why GST is slab-based per ticket, not a single flat rate
A flat GST rate on the whole booking is what a toy version of this would do — but it's not how Indian cinema ticket GST actually works: the applicable rate depends on the net price of each individual ticket (a low threshold band and a higher band above it), and the convenience fee is taxed separately at its own flat rate regardless of ticket price. Modeling this required computing GST after discount allocation and per tier line, using each line's post-discount price-per-ticket to pick its slab — because a discount can push one tier's effective price across the slab boundary while leaving another tier on the other side of it. Getting this right is exactly the kind of "messy real-world rule" the brief called out, and it's also why discount allocation had to happen before tax, not after: tax computed on the pre-discount gross would use the wrong slab entirely.

Why inventory validation happens before any reservation
price_booking checks every requested tier's availability up front, before reserving seats in any of them. Without this, a booking for "2 Silver + 3 Recliner" where only Silver has stock left would reserve the Silver seats, then fail on Recliner — leaving the counter with seats marked sold for a booking that never completed. Pricing and inventory are validated together, atomically, precisely because a half-booked state is worse than a rejected one.

Why the price-list import is a separate, defensive layer
The twist — a messy upstream price feed — is a data-quality problem, not a pricing problem, so it's handled as its own pass with its own report, before a Show ever gets built. Two design choices there matter more than the parsing regexes themselves:

Duplicate detection is case/whitespace-insensitive, but merging is price-aware. Two rows for "SILVER" and "Silver" at the same price are obviously the same tier reported twice — safe to merge. But two rows for "Gold" and "gold " at different prices are not safely mergeable: picking one price over the other is a business decision hiding inside what looks like a formatting cleanup. The engine refuses to guess — it keeps the first valid entry and rejects the conflicting one, and says exactly why, so a human resolves the conflict instead of the system silently picking a price.
Every rejection carries a specific, distinguishable reason (blank name vs. blank price vs. unparseable price vs. negative price vs. invalid seat count vs. price conflict) rather than a generic "invalid row." At a real counter, "why did this tier not show up," is the first question anyone asks when a report looks short, and a generic rejection reason is nearly as useless as no report at all.
What I deliberately left out
No persistence layer, no CLI, no web framework — the brief asked for a pricing engine the counter can trust, not a full booking system. Everything is plain functions and dataclasses so it's trivial to wire into whatever front end or database a real counter actually uses.
No currency other than INR, and no configurable currency symbol — the brief is explicitly about Indian GST slabs and rupee/paisa precision, so I optimized for getting that one currency exactly right rather than a half-generic abstraction over currencies I wasn't asked to support.
Offer stacking is currently exactly two offer types stacked in a fixed order, matching the brief precisely, rather than a generic rule engine for arbitrary offer combinations — that generality wasn't asked for, and building it speculatively would have traded clarity for flexibility nobody requested yet.
